# Pre-Mortem: Career Change

> Decision: "I'm going to quit my stable job at a big tech company to build a solo SaaS product"

---

**💥 The Executor**

I told myself I'd ship the MVP in three months. After all, I'd been building side projects for five years — how hard could full-time be? The first month was electric. I woke up at 7am, coded until midnight, felt alive for the first time in years.

By month two, I noticed something I hadn't expected: decision fatigue. At my old job, the product manager decided what to build. The designer handled the UI. The DevOps team managed deployments. Now every single decision — from the shade of blue on a button to the database schema — landed on me. I wasn't just coding; I was doing customer research, writing copy, handling billing integrations, managing a domain registrar, setting up transactional emails. I shipped less code in month two of "full-time" than I had in a good weekend of side-projecting.

By month four, the MVP was "almost done" — a state it would remain in for the next five months. I kept finding one more thing that needed to be right before launch. Looking back, I was afraid. Afraid that if I launched and nobody came, I'd have to face the possibility that the idea wasn't as good as I needed it to be.

I burned through my savings faster than projected. Not because of extravagance, but because I'd budgeted for "living expenses" and forgotten about health insurance, quarterly taxes, software subscriptions, and the small purchases I used to expense at work.

**⚠ Fatal Blind Spot:**
- I confused "being productive on nights and weekends" with "being productive full-time" — they are fundamentally different modes of work that require different energy and motivation management.
- I had no concept of how much invisible infrastructure my employer provided beyond a paycheck.

---

**🔍 The Analyst**

I missed the signal that was right in front of me the entire time: my side project had 340 signups, but I'd never once asked any of them to pay. I told myself the numbers would come after launch, after polish, after the "real" version. But willingness-to-sign-up and willingness-to-pay are separated by a canyon, not a step.

When I finally launched with a $29/month price tag, exactly 4 of those 340 users converted. Four. I panicked and dropped to $9/month. Eight more converted. Twelve paying customers at $9 per month: $108 in MRR against $4,200 in monthly expenses.

I'd made a classic mistake: I'd validated the problem ("yes, this is annoying") but not the solution's value ("yes, I'd pay for this specific fix"). The people who signed up were curious, not desperate. Curious users don't convert. Desperate users do.

The other thing I got wrong was the market size. I'd picked a niche because "niches are good," but I'd picked a niche of people who were technically sophisticated enough to build their own solution in a weekend — and many of them did, once they saw my approach.

**⚠ Fatal Blind Spot:**
- I treated signups as validation when they were just curiosity. I never tested the only metric that matters before quitting: someone giving me money.

---

**🔗 The Connector**

I never expected my partner to start resenting the decision by month three. We'd "discussed it" — but discussing a plan and living through its reality are different things. I was physically present but mentally absent. Every dinner conversation circled back to churn rates and feature requests. I stopped noticing their frustration until it became anger.

My former colleagues moved on faster than I imagined. Within six weeks, I was no longer in the loop on industry conversations. The Slack groups went quiet because I no longer had context on what they were discussing. My professional network, which I'd assumed was about me as a person, turned out to be largely about me as a node in a specific company's ecosystem.

When I started looking for a job again at month nine — trying to be casual about it, trying not to seem desperate — I discovered that the market had shifted. The hiring freezes I'd read about when I quit had deepened. The "I can always go back" safety net I'd assumed existed was full of holes.

**⚠ Fatal Blind Spot:**
- I treated my professional network as a permanent asset when it was actually a depreciating one — its value was tied to my position, not my person.
- I underestimated how asymmetrically this decision affected my partner, who bore the financial anxiety without the excitement of building something.

---

**✅ Decision Checklist:**
- [ ] Do I have 12+ months of personal runway with zero revenue, including health insurance and taxes?
- [ ] Have at least 5 strangers paid for this product (not just signed up)?
- [ ] Have I worked full-time on this for at least 2 consecutive weeks (vacation) to test my actual productivity?
- [ ] Has my partner/family explicitly agreed to the specific financial and lifestyle changes, not just the idea?
- [ ] Do I have a hard "return to employment" deadline that I will honor regardless of sunk cost?
- [ ] Is my target customer someone who can't easily build this themselves?
- [ ] Have I talked to at least 3 people who tried and failed at something similar?
